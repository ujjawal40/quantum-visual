Build an atom

Every atom is built from just three particles. The number of protons decides which element it is, the neutrons decide which version, and the electrons decide its charge. Add them one at a time and see.

Three particles make everything

Everything you can touch, from air to your own hand, is made of atoms. And every atom is made of the same three particles: protons and neutrons packed together in a tiny nucleus at the centre, and electrons spread around it.

Protons and neutrons weigh almost the same. An electron is nearly two thousand times lighter, so almost all of an atom's mass sits in its nucleus.

The proton count is the element

The number of protons is the atomic number, Z. It alone decides the element: one proton is always hydrogen, six is always carbon, twenty-six is always iron. Add one proton to carbon and it stops being carbon and becomes nitrogen.

Protons plus neutrons give the mass number, A:

A = Z + N Z = number of protons, N = number of neutrons. Electrons are too light to count.

We write an atom as its mass number above its symbol: 12C is carbon with 12 nucleons (6 protons and 6 neutrons).

Isotopes: same element, different weight

Atoms of one element can carry different numbers of neutrons. These versions are called isotopes. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons, carbon-13 has 7, and carbon-14 has 8. They behave the same in chemistry because chemistry depends on electrons, and the electron count follows the protons.

Not every combination holds together. Too many or too few neutrons and the nucleus is unstable: sooner or later it breaks apart, giving off radiation. Carbon-14 is unstable, which is what lets scientists date ancient bones and wood (lesson 6.4).

Ions: when electrons don't match

A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so the charges cancel. Remove an electron and the atom becomes a positive ion; add one and it becomes a negative ion.

charge = protons − electrons

Table salt is made of ions: sodium gives up one electron to become Na+, and chlorine takes one to become Cl−. The opposite charges hold the crystal together.

Electrons fill shells

For the first twenty elements, a simple rule tells you where the electrons go: the first shell holds 2, the second holds 8, the third holds 8, and the next 2 go into a fourth. The outermost electrons decide how an atom reacts. Atoms with full outer shells, like neon and argon, hardly react at all.

Shells are a simplified picture. In Module 5 you will see that electrons really form clouds of probability called orbitals.

How small is the nucleus?

An atom is about 10−10 m across, but its nucleus is only about 10−15 m: one hundred thousand times smaller. If an atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be a marble on the centre spot, holding more than 99.9% of the mass. The rest is almost entirely empty space.

Newton vs quantum

What Newton predicts

A miniature solar system: the Coulomb pull of the nucleus replaces gravity, and electrons circle it like planets on any orbit they like. But a circling charge radiates, so each electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in about 10−11 s.

What actually happens

Atoms last for billions of years. Electrons sit in fixed shells with fixed energies and do not spiral in. A shell is not a track but a region where the electron is likely to be found.

Where they meet: Newton and Coulomb still explain why the nucleus holds its electrons, and why opposite charges attract. What they cannot explain is why atoms are stable. Module 3 adds the missing rule.
