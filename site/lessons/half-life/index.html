Half-life

Nobody can say when a single radioactive nucleus will decay. Yet a sample of millions shrinks so predictably that we use it as a clock to date bones, rocks and the Earth itself.

Nuclei that fall apart

Some nuclei hold too many protons or neutrons to be stable. Sooner or later each one throws out an alpha particle, a beta particle or a gamma ray, and becomes a different nucleus. This is radioactive decay. The nucleus you start with is the parent; what it turns into is the daughter.

One nucleus: pure chance

Pick any single nucleus and ask when it will decay. There is no answer. It might go in the next second or wait a thousand years, and nothing about it tells you which. Physicists have looked hard for a hidden clock inside the nucleus, and found none.

Stranger still, a nucleus does not age. One that has survived a million years is exactly as likely to decay in the next second as one made a moment ago. Click a single square in the simulation and watch it: you cannot tell when it will flash.

Many nuclei: a clock

Chance becomes certainty in large numbers. Every isotope has a half-life, T½: the time in which each nucleus has a 50% chance of decaying. Start with N0 nuclei, and after one half-life about half are left; after two, a quarter; after three, an eighth.

N = N0 × (½)t / T½ N is the number left after time t.

Iodine-131, used to treat thyroid disease, has a half-life of 8.02 days. Start with 800 nuclei and after 24 days (three half-lives) about 800 × ⅛ = 100 remain. With 800 the answer is only roughly 100; with 800 million it would be extremely close to one eighth.

The decay constant

The same rule is often written with the decay constant λ, the chance per second that one nucleus decays:

N = N0 e−λt,    λ = ln 2T½ ≈ 0.693T½

The activity, the number of decays each second, is A = λN. A sample with a short half-life is fiercely active but soon gone; one with a long half-life is gentle but lasts.

  • Radon-222, 3.82 days: a gas that seeps out of rocks into basements.
  • Iodine-131, 8.02 days: treats an overactive thyroid.
  • Cobalt-60, 5.27 years: sterilises medical equipment and food.
  • Carbon-14, 5,730 years: dates things that were once alive.
  • Uranium-238, 4.47 billion years: about as old as the Earth, so half of the Earth's original uranium-238 is still here.

Carbon dating

Cosmic rays keep making carbon-14 in the air. Plants take it in, animals eat the plants, and every living thing holds the same tiny fraction of carbon-14. When it dies, the intake stops and the carbon-14 starts to run down.

Measure what fraction is left and you know how many half-lives have passed. A bone with half its carbon-14 is about 5,730 years old; with a quarter, two half-lives. Beyond about 50,000 years too little is left to measure, so older things are dated with longer-lived isotopes such as potassium-40 or uranium-238.

Medicine

Hospitals choose isotopes by half-life. A tracer for a scan should last a few hours: long enough to image, short enough to leave the body quickly. A source for radiotherapy should last years so it need not be replaced often.
