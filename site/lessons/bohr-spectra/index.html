Jumps and photons

A hydrogen atom can only hold certain amounts of energy. When its electron drops from one level to another, it throws out a single photon, and that photon's colour tells you exactly how far it fell.

Electrons live on a ladder

In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed that the electron in hydrogen can only sit on certain orbits, each with its own energy. Between the rungs there is nothing: the electron is never found with an in-between energy.

En = − 13.6 eVn2 n = 1, 2, 3, … is the level number. Negative means the electron is bound to the proton.

The ground state, n = 1, sits at −13.6 eV. Level 2 is at −3.40 eV, level 3 at −1.51 eV, and the rungs crowd together as they approach 0 eV, where the electron breaks free.

Every jump makes one photon

An excited electron does not stay put. It falls back, and the energy it loses leaves as one photon:

ΔE = hf = hcλ   so   λ = 1240 eV·nmΔE

For the drop from level 3 to level 2, ΔE = 3.40 − 1.51 = 1.89 eV, so λ = 1240 ÷ 1.89 ≈ 656 nm. That is red light, the glow of hydrogen in a discharge tube and in nebulae across the sky.

Big jumps give high-energy, short-wavelength photons. Small jumps give low-energy, long-wavelength ones. Many jumps make light our eyes cannot see.

Hydrogen's fingerprint

Jumps are grouped by the level they end on. Each group is a series, named after the person who first measured it:

  • Lyman (ending on n = 1): all ultraviolet, 91–122 nm.
  • Balmer (ending on n = 2): four visible lines at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm, then more in the ultraviolet.
  • Paschen (ending on n = 3): all infrared, from 820 nm upward.

No other element makes this exact set of lines. Astronomers read them in starlight to tell what stars are made of.

Absorbing is fussy

The reverse also works: an atom can swallow a photon and jump up. But only a photon whose energy matches a gap exactly. A photon with slightly too much or too little energy passes straight through. Try it with the photon gun.

A photon with 13.6 eV or more (λ ≤ 91.2 nm) does something different: it knocks the electron out of the atom entirely. That is ionisation.

How fast is the electron?

On Bohr's orbits the electron's speed is

vn = 2.19 × 106 m/sn about c/137 in the ground state: fast, but not close to light speed.

That is around 7,900,000 km/h, fast enough to circle the Earth in about 18 seconds. Watch the speed readout change as the electron moves between levels.

Newton vs quantum

What Newton predicts

An electron orbiting a proton could have any radius and any energy. As it radiates, it should spiral inward continuously, giving off a smear of all colours, and crash into the nucleus in about 10−11 s.

What actually happens

Only certain energies are allowed. Atoms are stable, and hydrogen emits sharp lines at exact wavelengths instead of a smear.

Where they meet: Bohr kept Newton's force balance, mv²/r = ke²/r², and added one quantum rule. That is why Newton gives the right speed here. For very large n the rungs crowd so close together that the energy looks continuous again.
