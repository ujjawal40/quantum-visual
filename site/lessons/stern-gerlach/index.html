Spin: up or down

Fire silver atoms through a lopsided magnet and they land in exactly two spots, never anywhere in between. Every electron carries a tiny magnet that can only ever be measured pointing one of two ways.

Every electron is a tiny magnet

An electron carries a built-in amount of angular momentum called spin, and with it a small magnetic moment, like a compass needle. The name is a leftover picture: if an electron really were a spinning ball, its surface would have to move faster than light. Spin is simply a property electrons have, like charge or mass.

What makes spin strange is what happens when you measure it. Pick any direction. The spin along that direction always comes out as one of exactly two values:

Sz = ms ħ,   ms = +½ or −½ "Spin up" and "spin down". There is no third answer, and nothing in between.

The 1922 experiment

In Frankfurt, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach heated silver in an oven and let a thin beam of atoms fly through a magnet with one pointed pole and one flat pole. Near the pointed pole the field is stronger, so the field is non-uniform. A tiny magnet in a non-uniform field is pushed toward or away from the strong side, depending on which way it points.

A silver atom has 47 electrons, and all but one are paired up so their magnets cancel. The single outer electron sets the atom's magnetism. So each silver atom is a test of one electron's spin.

If the atoms' magnets pointed every which way, as compass needles would, the beam should spread into a smear. Stern and Gerlach found two separate spots on their glass plate. Switch the simulation to Classical magnets, then to Quantum spin, and compare.

Measuring changes the answer

Take the atoms that came out "up" from a Z magnet and send them through a second Z magnet. Every one comes out up again: the first measurement left them in a definite state.

Now send those up atoms through an X magnet, turned 90°. They split 50/50 between +x and −x. Knowing the spin along z tells you nothing about the spin along x.

The real surprise comes with a third magnet. Keep only the +x atoms and measure z again. You might expect them all to be up, since you already selected up. They aren't: half come out down. The x measurement wiped out the z information. In quantum physics, a measurement is not a peek at a value that was already there; it changes the state.

For a magnet tilted at angle θ to the direction the spin was last measured, the chance of "up" is

P(up) = cos2(θ/2) θ = 0°: always up. θ = 90°: half and half. θ = 180°: never up.

Why it matters

Spin is why chemistry works. Each orbital can hold two electrons only because there are two spin states: one up, one down (lesson 5.5, Pauli exclusion). It is also how MRI scanners see inside the body, using the spins of protons in water.

And it is the simplest quantum computer. A spin-½ particle is a qubit: before measurement it can be in a blend of up and down, and the cos²(θ/2) rule is exactly how a qubit's measurement probabilities work.

Newton vs quantum

What Newton predicts

Each atom is a tiny spinning top whose magnet can point in any direction. The push it feels depends smoothly on that angle, so the beam spreads into one continuous smear from top to bottom.

What actually happens

Exactly two spots, every time, along whatever direction you measure. Angular momentum comes in fixed steps of ħ, and an electron's spin has only two possible measured values.

Where they meet: a large spinning object, like a top or a planet, has an angular momentum of trillions of trillions of ħ. Its allowed values are so close together that they look continuous, and Newton's smooth picture works perfectly.
