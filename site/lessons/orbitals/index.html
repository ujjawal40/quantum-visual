Orbital shapes in 3D

The electron in an atom has no path. Quantum physics tells us only where it is likely to be, and that likelihood has a shape: a ball, a dumbbell, a clover. Those shapes are orbitals, and they are what chemistry is built from.

From orbits to clouds

Bohr pictured the electron circling the nucleus like a planet. It was a good start, but it fails for every atom except hydrogen, and it gets the shapes of molecules wrong.

In the full quantum theory the electron is described by a wavefunction, ψ (psi). Where ψ² is large, you are likely to find the electron when you look; where it is small, you rarely will. If you could photograph one hydrogen atom thousands of times and stack the pictures, the dots would build up a cloud. That cloud is the orbital.

Each dot in the simulation is one possible place the electron could be found. Dense regions are likely, sparse ones unlikely.

Three numbers give the address

Every orbital in hydrogen is labelled by three whole numbers:

  • n, the shell (1, 2, 3, …), sets the energy and the size. Bigger n, bigger cloud.
  • l, from 0 to n − 1, sets the shape. Chemists use letters: l = 0 is s, 1 is p, 2 is d, 3 is f.
  • ml, from −l to +l, sets which way the shape points.

So a shell with number n holds n² orbitals: one in shell 1, four in shell 2 (2s and three 2p), nine in shell 3.

En = − 13.6 eVn2 In hydrogen the energy depends only on n, so 2s and all three 2p orbitals have the same energy, −3.40 eV.

Nodes: where the electron is never found

A node is a surface where ψ = 0, so the electron is never found there. There are two kinds:

  • Angular nodes are flat planes or cones through the nucleus. A p orbital has one plane between its two lobes. An orbital has l angular nodes.
  • Radial nodes are spheres. The 2s orbital is a small ball inside a bigger shell, with an empty sphere between them. An orbital has n − l − 1 radial nodes.

Add them up and every orbital has n − 1 nodes in total. Radial nodes hide inside the cloud, so switch on Slice to cut the cloud open and see them.

Phase: the sign of ψ

ψ can be positive or negative. Square it and the sign disappears, so it does not change where the electron is found. But it matters a great deal when two atoms meet: lobes with the same sign overlap to make a bond, opposite signs cancel. With Colour by phase on, blue dots are where ψ is positive and orange where it is negative. A node is always where the colour flips.

Shapes worth knowing

  • s orbitals are spheres. 1s is one ball; 2s and 3s add shells.
  • p orbitals are dumbbells along x, y or z, three per shell from n = 2.
  • d orbitals are mostly four-leaf clovers, five per shell from n = 3. The odd one out, dz², is two lobes with a ring around its waist.

Each orbital, whatever its shape, holds at most two electrons: one with spin +½ and one with spin −½.

Newton vs quantum

What Newton predicts

A planet has a definite position and velocity at every moment, so it traces a path. Treat the electron the same way and it should sit on a circle of fixed radius.

What actually happens

The electron has no path, only a cloud of likely positions. In the 1s orbital it can be found close to the nucleus or far away, in any direction.

Where they meet: the single most likely distance for the 1s electron is exactly 0.0529 nm, the radius of Bohr's orbit. The classical orbit survives as the peak of the cloud.
