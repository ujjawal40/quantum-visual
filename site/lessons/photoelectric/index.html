The photoelectric effect

Shine light on a metal and electrons can fly off it. But only the right colour works: the dimmest violet light frees electrons that the brightest red light never can. Explaining why won Einstein his Nobel prize.

What the wave picture expects

By 1900 everyone agreed light was a wave. A wave carries energy spread across its whole front, and a brighter wave carries more. So the wave picture made three clear predictions for light falling on a metal:

  • Any colour should free electrons, if it is bright enough.
  • Brighter light should throw electrons out faster.
  • Very dim light should need time to build up enough energy before any electron leaves.

Experiments, most carefully by Philipp Lenard in 1902, showed all three are wrong. Try them yourself in the simulation before reading on.

What actually happens

Below a certain frequency, no electrons come out at all, however bright the light. Above it, electrons come out instantly, even from the dimmest light. Turning up the brightness makes more electrons, but not faster ones. Only changing the colour changes their speed.

Einstein's photons

In 1905 Albert Einstein took Planck's idea seriously: light arrives in packets, called photons, each carrying an energy set only by its frequency.

E = hf = hcλ h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV·s. Brightness is the number of photons, not the energy of each.

One photon gives all its energy to one electron. Part of it is spent pulling the electron free of the metal. That cost is the work function, φ, different for each metal. Whatever is left becomes the electron's kinetic energy:

KEmax = hf − φ "max" because electrons deeper in the metal lose a little extra on the way out.

If hf is less than φ, no single photon can free an electron, and a million weak photons do not add up: each one is absorbed alone. That explains the cut-off.

The threshold

Setting KEmax = 0 gives the lowest frequency that works, the threshold:

f0 = φh   or   λ0 = 1240 eV·nmφ

Caesium has φ = 2.1 eV, so its threshold is 1240 ÷ 2.1 ≈ 590 nm: even yellow light works. Copper has φ = 4.7 eV, so it needs ultraviolet shorter than 264 nm. This is why light meters and old camera tubes used caesium.

Measuring the energy with a stopping voltage

To measure how fast the electrons leave, make the collector negative. Electrons must then climb an electric hill to reach it. Raise the voltage until the current just stops. At that stopping voltage Vs, even the fastest electrons are turned back:

e Vs = KEmax A stopping voltage of 1.5 V means the fastest electrons had 1.5 eV.

Plot KEmax against frequency for several colours and you get a straight line. Its slope is Planck's constant h, the same for every metal. Its intercept is −φ. Robert Millikan did exactly this in 1916, hoping to prove Einstein wrong, and instead measured h to within 0.5%.

Why it won a Nobel prize

Einstein's 1921 Nobel prize was awarded for this explanation, not for relativity. It was the first strong evidence that light comes in quanta, and it opened the road to quantum physics. Today the same effect runs solar cells, camera sensors and the night-vision tubes in telescopes.

Classical waves vs photons

What wave theory predicts

Energy depends on brightness. Bright red light should free electrons, brighter light should make them faster, and dim light should need a delay to soak up enough energy.

What actually happens

Energy per photon depends only on frequency. Below the threshold nothing happens at any brightness; above it, electrons leave instantly, and brightness only changes how many.

Where they meet: a bright beam carries an enormous number of photons. Averaged together they behave like the smooth classical wave, which is why wave optics still works perfectly for lenses, interference and radio.
