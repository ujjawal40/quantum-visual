1Foundations

Light comes in packets, matter behaves like waves, and the hydrogen atom has a ladder of energies. Algebra and a little calculus are all you need.

Photon energy

Light is delivered in packets called photons, and the energy of each packet is set by the light's frequency, that is, by its colour.

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad hc \approx 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}$$

Symbols

\(E\)
energy of one photon, J (often quoted in eV; 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J)
\(h\)
Planck's constant, 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
\(f\)
frequency of the light, Hz
\(c\)
speed of light, 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s
\(\lambda\)
wavelength, m

Derivation

Postulate. \(E = hf\) is not derived from anything older. Planck guessed it in 1900 to explain the glow of hot objects, and Einstein took it seriously in 1905 to explain the photoelectric effect. It is checked by experiment.

  1. Planck's rule: light of frequency \(f\) can only give or take energy in whole packets of size \(hf\).
  2. Every wave obeys speed = frequency × wavelength, so for light \(c = f\lambda\), which gives \(f = c/\lambda\).
  3. Substitute: $$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}.$$
  4. A handy number: \(hc = (6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8}) = 1.986\times10^{-25}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{m}\). Divide by \(1.602\times10^{-19}\) J/eV and by \(10^{-9}\) m/nm to get \(hc \approx 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}\). Now \(E\,[\text{eV}] = 1240 / \lambda\,[\text{nm}]\).

Check with numbers

Green light, \(\lambda = 500\) nm: \(E = 1240/500 = 2.48\) eV \(= 3.97\times10^{-19}\) J. A 1 W green laser therefore sends out \(1/(3.97\times10^{-19}) \approx 2.5\times10^{18}\) photons every second.

Lesson 2.4: The photoelectric effect

Photon momentum

A photon has no mass, yet it carries momentum. That is why light can push on things.

$$p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}$$

Symbols

\(p\)
momentum of one photon, kg·m/s
\(E\)
photon energy, J
\(m\)
rest mass, kg (zero for a photon)
\(c,\ h,\ \lambda\)
speed of light, Planck's constant, wavelength

Derivation

  1. Special relativity links energy, momentum and rest mass for any particle: $$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2.$$
  2. A photon has \(m = 0\), so \(E^2 = (pc)^2\), and since both are positive, \(E = pc\).
  3. Rearrange and use \(E = hc/\lambda\): $$p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{hc/\lambda}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}.$$

Check with numbers

A 500 nm photon: \(p = 6.626\times10^{-34}/(5.00\times10^{-7}) = 1.33\times10^{-27}\) kg·m/s. Tiny, but a solar sail near Earth catches about \(4\times10^{21}\) photons per square metre each second, and the pushes add up.

Photoelectric equation

One photon frees at most one electron. The electron leaves with whatever energy is left after paying the metal's exit fee.

$$K_{\max} = hf - \phi, \qquad eV_s = K_{\max}$$

Symbols

\(K_{\max}\)
largest kinetic energy of an emitted electron, J or eV
\(hf\)
energy of one incoming photon
\(\phi\)
work function: the least energy needed to pull an electron out of the metal, J or eV
\(e\)
elementary charge, 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
\(V_s\)
stopping potential: the reverse voltage that just stops the fastest electrons, V

Derivation

  1. Energy is conserved when one photon is absorbed by one electron: the electron gains \(hf\).
  2. Escaping costs at least \(\phi\) (more for electrons that start deeper in the metal). The least-bound electrons keep the most: $$K_{\max} = hf - \phi.$$
  3. If \(hf \lt \phi\), no electron escapes however bright the light is. The threshold frequency is \(f_0 = \phi/h\).
  4. To stop the fastest electron, a reverse voltage must remove all its kinetic energy. Work done by the field is \(eV_s\), so \(eV_s = K_{\max}\), giving $$V_s = \frac{h}{e}\,f - \frac{\phi}{e}.$$ A graph of \(V_s\) against \(f\) is a straight line with slope \(h/e\): this is how Millikan measured Planck's constant.

Check with numbers

Sodium, \(\phi = 2.28\) eV, lit with 400 nm violet light: \(hf = 1240/400 = 3.10\) eV, so \(K_{\max} = 3.10 - 2.28 = 0.82\) eV and \(V_s = 0.82\) V. Red light at 700 nm has \(hf = 1.77\) eV \(\lt \phi\): no electrons at all.

Lesson 2.4: The photoelectric effect

De Broglie wavelength

Everything that moves has a wavelength. The more momentum it has, the shorter the wave.

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}\ \text{(electron accelerated through } V\text{)}$$

Symbols

\(\lambda\)
de Broglie wavelength, m
\(p\)
momentum, kg·m/s
\(m\)
mass of the particle, kg (electron: 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg)
\(V\)
accelerating voltage, V
\(e,\ h\)
elementary charge, Planck's constant

Derivation

Postulate. In 1924 de Broglie proposed that the photon rule \(p = h/\lambda\) works backwards for matter. Davisson and Germer confirmed it in 1927 by diffracting electrons off a nickel crystal.

  1. For light, \(p = h/\lambda\). Assume the same holds for any particle: \(\lambda = h/p\).
  2. An electron starting from rest and accelerated through a voltage \(V\) gains kinetic energy \(K = eV\).
  3. At everyday speeds \(K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = p^2/2m\), so \(p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{2meV}\).
  4. Substitute: $$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}} \approx \frac{1.226\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{V\,[\text{volts}]}}.$$

Check with numbers

An electron through 100 V: \(\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.602\times10^{-19})(100)} = 5.40\times10^{-24}\) kg·m/s, so \(\lambda = 6.626\times10^{-34}/5.40\times10^{-24} = 1.23\times10^{-10}\) m \(= 0.123\) nm, about the spacing of atoms in a crystal. A 0.15 kg cricket ball at 40 m/s has \(\lambda \approx 1\times10^{-34}\) m: far too small to ever see.

Lesson 4.3: The double-slit experiment

Bohr model of hydrogen

If the electron's angular momentum can only come in whole steps of ħ, hydrogen gets a ladder of allowed orbits and energies.

$$r_n = n^2 a_0, \qquad v_n = \frac{\alpha c}{n}, \qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}$$

Symbols

\(n\)
level number, 1, 2, 3, …
\(r_n\)
orbit radius, m
\(a_0\)
Bohr radius, \(\hbar^2/(m_e k e^2) = 5.29\times10^{-11}\) m = 0.0529 nm
\(v_n\)
electron speed, m/s
\(\alpha\)
fine-structure constant, \(ke^2/(\hbar c) \approx 1/137\), no unit
\(E_n\)
total energy of level \(n\), eV; negative means bound
\(k\)
Coulomb constant, 8.988 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
\(\hbar\)
\(h/2\pi = 1.055\times10^{-34}\) J·s
\(m_e\)
electron mass, 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg

Derivation

Part postulate. Bohr's quantisation rule \(m_e v r = n\hbar\) was a bold guess in 1913. It gives exactly the right energies for hydrogen, but the picture of a circular orbit is wrong; the Schrödinger equation (tier 4) replaces it.

  1. The Coulomb pull provides the centripetal force: $$\frac{m_e v^2}{r} = \frac{ke^2}{r^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad m_e v^2 = \frac{ke^2}{r}.$$
  2. Bohr's rule: \(m_e v r = n\hbar\), so \(v = n\hbar/(m_e r)\).
  3. Substitute into step 1: \(m_e \dfrac{n^2\hbar^2}{m_e^2 r^2} = \dfrac{ke^2}{r}\), which gives $$r_n = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{m_e k e^2} = n^2 a_0.$$
  4. Speed: \(v_n = \dfrac{n\hbar}{m_e r_n} = \dfrac{ke^2}{n\hbar} = \dfrac{1}{n}\cdot\dfrac{ke^2}{\hbar c}\,c = \dfrac{\alpha c}{n}.\)
  5. Energy is kinetic plus potential: \(E = \tfrac12 m_e v^2 - \dfrac{ke^2}{r}\). Step 1 says \(\tfrac12 m_e v^2 = \dfrac{ke^2}{2r}\), so $$E_n = -\frac{ke^2}{2r_n} = -\frac{m_e k^2 e^4}{2\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2}.$$
  6. The constant is \(\dfrac{ke^2}{2a_0} = \dfrac{1.440\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{2 \times 0.0529\ \text{nm}} = 13.6\) eV.

Check with numbers

Level 2: \(r_2 = 4 \times 0.0529 = 0.212\) nm, \(v_2 = (2.998\times10^8)/(137 \times 2) = 1.09\times10^6\) m/s, \(E_2 = -13.6/4 = -3.40\) eV. Freeing the electron from the ground state takes \(+13.6\) eV, matching hydrogen's measured ionisation energy.

Lesson 3.4: Jumps and photons

Rydberg formula

When hydrogen's electron drops from one level to a lower one, it gives out a photon whose wavelength is fixed by the two level numbers.

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right), \qquad R = \frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{hc} = 1.097\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}$$

Symbols

\(\lambda\)
wavelength of the emitted photon, m
\(n_1,\ n_2\)
lower and upper level numbers, \(n_2 \gt n_1\)
\(R\)
Rydberg constant, m⁻¹

Derivation

  1. Energy is conserved: the photon carries away the energy the atom loses, \(hf = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1}\).
  2. Use \(E_n = -E_R/n^2\) with \(E_R = 13.6\) eV: $$\frac{hc}{\lambda} = -\frac{E_R}{n_2^2} + \frac{E_R}{n_1^2} = E_R\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right).$$
  3. Divide by \(hc\) and call \(R = E_R/hc = 13.6/1240\ \text{nm}^{-1} = 0.01097\ \text{nm}^{-1}\).
  4. Drops to \(n_1 = 1\) form the Lyman series (ultraviolet), to \(n_1 = 2\) the Balmer series (visible), to \(n_1 = 3\) Paschen (infrared).

Check with numbers

Red Balmer line, \(3 \to 2\): \(1/\lambda = 0.01097\,(1/4 - 1/9) = 0.01097 \times 5/36\), so \(\lambda = 656\) nm. Lyman-α, \(2 \to 1\): \(\lambda = 4/(3 \times 0.01097) = 121.5\) nm with energy 10.2 eV (measured: 121.6 nm; the tiny gap is because the proton recoils a little).

Radioactive decay and half-life

Each nucleus decays at random, but a large crowd of them always halves in the same time.

$$N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

Symbols

\(N(t)\)
number of undecayed nuclei at time \(t\)
\(N_0\)
number at \(t = 0\)
\(\lambda\)
decay constant: probability per second that one nucleus decays, s⁻¹ (not a wavelength here)
\(T_{1/2}\)
half-life, s

Derivation

  1. Assume every nucleus has the same small chance \(\lambda\,dt\) of decaying in a short time \(dt\), independent of its age and of the others. Out of \(N\) nuclei, about \(\lambda N\,dt\) decay: $$dN = -\lambda N\,dt.$$
  2. Separate the variables and integrate from \(0\) to \(t\): $$\int_{N_0}^{N}\frac{dN'}{N'} = -\lambda\int_0^t dt' \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ln\frac{N}{N_0} = -\lambda t.$$
  3. Take the exponential: \(N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}\).
  4. Half-life: set \(N = N_0/2\). Then \(\tfrac12 = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}\), so \(\lambda T_{1/2} = \ln 2\), giving \(T_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda \approx 0.693/\lambda\).
  5. The same law in half-lives: \(N = N_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}\).

Check with numbers

Carbon-14 has \(T_{1/2} = 5730\) years, so \(\lambda = 0.693/5730 = 1.21\times10^{-4}\) per year. A bone with 30% of its original carbon-14 left is \(t = T_{1/2}\log_2(1/0.30) = 5730 \times 1.74 \approx 9950\) years old.

E = mc² and mass defect

Energy has mass. A nucleus weighs less than its separate protons and neutrons, and the missing mass is the energy that binds it.

$$E = mc^2, \qquad E_{\text{binding}} = \Delta m\,c^2,\quad 1\ \text{u} \leftrightarrow 931.5\ \text{MeV}$$

Symbols

\(E\)
energy, J
\(m\)
mass, kg
\(\Delta m\)
mass defect: (mass of separate nucleons) − (mass of nucleus), kg or u
u
atomic mass unit, 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

Derivation

Einstein's 1906 "box" argument, which needs only the photon momentum \(p = E/c\) and the fact that a closed system's centre of mass cannot move by itself.

  1. A closed box of mass \(M\) and length \(L\) sits at rest in empty space. The left wall emits a flash of light of energy \(E\) towards the right wall.
  2. The light carries momentum \(E/c\), so the box recoils to the left with speed \(v = E/(Mc)\).
  3. The light crosses in time \(t \approx L/c\) (the box barely moves), then is absorbed and the box stops. The box has moved \(\Delta x = -vt = -\dfrac{EL}{Mc^2}\).
  4. Nothing outside pushed the box, so its centre of mass cannot have moved. The only way out is that the light carried some mass \(m\) from left to right: $$mL + M\Delta x = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad mL = \frac{EL}{c^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad m = \frac{E}{c^2}.$$
  5. Turned around: removing energy \(E\) from any object lowers its mass by \(E/c^2\). When nucleons bind, they release energy, so the nucleus is lighter by \(\Delta m = E_{\text{binding}}/c^2\).
  6. Unit shortcut: \(1\ \text{u} \times c^2 = (1.6605\times10^{-27})(2.998\times10^8)^2 = 1.4924\times10^{-10}\) J \(= 931.5\) MeV.

Check with numbers

Helium-4: two protons and two neutrons weigh \(2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882\) u. The nucleus weighs 4.001506 u. \(\Delta m = 0.030376\) u, so \(E_{\text{binding}} = 0.030376 \times 931.5 = 28.3\) MeV, about 7.1 MeV per nucleon.

2Waves and uncertainty

Treat particles as waves and three things follow: interference, quantised energies in a box, and a limit on how sharply anything can be known.

Double-slit fringes

Waves from two slits add up where their paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths, making evenly spaced bright fringes.

$$d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad \Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}$$

Symbols

\(d\)
distance between the slit centres, m
\(\theta\)
angle from the centre line to a bright fringe
\(m\)
fringe order, 0, ±1, ±2, …
\(\lambda\)
wavelength (de Broglie wavelength for electrons), m
\(L\)
distance from slits to screen, m
\(\Delta y\)
spacing between neighbouring bright fringes, m

Derivation

  1. For a distant screen, the two paths to a point at angle \(\theta\) are almost parallel. The lower path is longer by \(d\sin\theta\).
  2. The waves arrive in step, and add, when this extra path is a whole number of wavelengths: \(d\sin\theta = m\lambda\). They cancel when it is a half-number.
  3. The fringe's height on the screen is \(y = L\tan\theta\). For small angles \(\tan\theta \approx \sin\theta \approx \theta\), so \(y_m = m\lambda L/d\).
  4. Neighbouring fringes are \(\Delta y = y_{m+1} - y_m = \lambda L/d\) apart.
  5. For electrons, use \(\lambda = h/p\). The pattern builds up one dot at a time: the probability of a dot is \(|\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2\), not \(|\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2\).

Check with numbers

A red laser, \(\lambda = 633\) nm, slits \(d = 0.10\) mm apart, screen at \(L = 1.0\) m: \(\Delta y = (633\times10^{-9})(1.0)/(1.0\times10^{-4}) = 6.3\) mm.

Particle in a box

A particle trapped between two walls can only have certain energies, because a whole number of half-wavelengths must fit between the walls.

$$E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}, \qquad \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

Symbols

\(n\)
quantum number, 1, 2, 3, …
\(m\)
mass of the particle, kg
\(L\)
width of the box, m
\(\psi_n(x)\)
wavefunction, m⁻¹ᐟ²
\(V(x)\)
potential energy: 0 inside, infinite at and beyond the walls

Derivation

  1. Inside the box \(V = 0\), so the time-independent Schrödinger equation (see stationary states) is $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -k^2\psi,\quad k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}.$$
  2. The general solution is \(\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx\).
  3. The walls are infinitely high, so \(\psi = 0\) there. At \(x = 0\): \(B = 0\). At \(x = L\): \(A\sin kL = 0\). \(A = 0\) would mean no particle, so \(\sin kL = 0\), giving \(kL = n\pi\).
  4. Then \(\dfrac{2mE}{\hbar^2} = \dfrac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\), so $$E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8mL^2}\quad(\hbar = h/2\pi).$$ \(n = 0\) is ruled out because then \(\psi = 0\) everywhere: the particle can never sit still.
  5. Normalise so the total probability is 1: \(\int_0^L A^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = A^2 L/2 = 1\), so \(A = \sqrt{2/L}\).

Check with numbers

An electron in a box \(L = 1.0\) nm wide: \(E_1 = (6.626\times10^{-34})^2/(8 \times 9.109\times10^{-31} \times 10^{-18}) = 6.03\times10^{-20}\) J \(= 0.376\) eV. Then \(E_2 = 1.50\) eV and \(E_3 = 3.38\) eV. This is roughly why small dye molecules and quantum dots have colours.

Uncertainty principle

No state has both a sharp position and a sharp momentum. The product of the two spreads can never be smaller than ħ/2.

$$\sigma_x\,\sigma_p \ \ge\ \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

Symbols

\(\sigma_x\)
standard deviation of position measurements, m
\(\sigma_p\)
standard deviation of momentum measurements, kg·m/s
\(\hat{x},\ \hat{p}\)
position and momentum operators; \(\hat{p} = -i\hbar\,\dfrac{d}{dx}\)
\(\langle f|g\rangle\)
inner product \(\int f^*g\,dx\)

Derivation

This is the Robertson derivation. It needs complex numbers and the idea of an inner product.

  1. Define the shifted wavefunctions \(f = (\hat{x} - \langle x\rangle)\psi\) and \(g = (\hat{p} - \langle p\rangle)\psi\). Then \(\sigma_x^2 = \langle f|f\rangle\) and \(\sigma_p^2 = \langle g|g\rangle\).
  2. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \(\langle f|f\rangle\langle g|g\rangle \ge |\langle f|g\rangle|^2\), and the fact that any complex number \(z\) has \(|z|^2 \ge (\operatorname{Im} z)^2\), give $$\sigma_x^2\sigma_p^2 \ \ge\ \left(\frac{\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle}{2i}\right)^2.$$
  3. Expanding, the \(\langle x\rangle\langle p\rangle\) terms cancel and \(\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle = \langle \hat{x}\hat{p} - \hat{p}\hat{x}\rangle = \langle[\hat{x},\hat{p}]\rangle\).
  4. Compute the commutator on any function \(\phi\): $$[\hat{x},\hat{p}]\phi = x(-i\hbar\phi') + i\hbar(x\phi)' = -i\hbar x\phi' + i\hbar\phi + i\hbar x\phi' = i\hbar\phi.$$
  5. So \(\sigma_x^2\sigma_p^2 \ge (i\hbar/2i)^2 = (\hbar/2)^2\), and taking square roots, \(\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2\).
  6. Equality: a Gaussian \(\psi \propto e^{-x^2/4\sigma^2}\) has \(\sigma_x = \sigma\) and \(\sigma_p = \hbar/2\sigma\), so the product is exactly \(\hbar/2\). Gaussians are the "least uncertain" states, which is why the wave packet lab starts with one.

Check with numbers

Confine an electron to \(\sigma_x = 0.1\) nm, about the size of an atom: \(\sigma_p \ge 1.055\times10^{-34}/(2\times10^{-10}) = 5.3\times10^{-25}\) kg·m/s, so its speed is uncertain by at least \(5.3\times10^{-25}/9.109\times10^{-31} \approx 5.8\times10^5\) m/s. That is why electrons in atoms move so fast.

3The Schrödinger equation

One equation tells a wavefunction how to change in time. Everything in this tier follows from it. Complex numbers and partial derivatives appear from here on.

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation

The quantum version of F = ma: given the wavefunction now, it tells you the wavefunction a moment later.

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\nabla^2\psi + V\psi \;=\; \hat{H}\psi$$

Symbols

\(\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\)
wavefunction, a complex number at every point; \(|\psi|^2\) is probability density, m⁻³ in 3D
\(i\)
\(\sqrt{-1}\)
\(\hbar\)
reduced Planck constant, 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
\(m\)
mass of the particle, kg
\(\nabla^2\)
\(\partial^2/\partial x^2 + \partial^2/\partial y^2 + \partial^2/\partial z^2\), m⁻²
\(V(\mathbf{r})\)
potential energy, J
\(\hat{H}\)
Hamiltonian: the energy operator, kinetic plus potential

Where it comes from

Postulate. The Schrödinger equation cannot be derived from older physics, just as Newton's laws cannot. What follows is the reasoning Schrödinger used to guess it in 1926. It is accepted because every prediction checked since then has agreed with experiment.

  1. A free particle with definite momentum should be a wave with \(p = \hbar k\) (de Broglie) and \(E = \hbar\omega\) (Planck). The simplest such wave is $$\psi = A\,e^{i(kx - \omega t)}.$$
  2. Differentiating picks out energy and momentum: $$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \hbar\omega\,\psi = E\psi, \qquad -i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = \hbar k\,\psi = p\psi.$$ So energy becomes the operator \(i\hbar\,\partial/\partial t\) and momentum becomes \(-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x\).
  3. For a free particle \(E = p^2/2m\). Apply the operators: \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = \dfrac{1}{2m}(-i\hbar\,\partial_x)^2\psi = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi\). Check: both sides give \(\hbar\omega\psi\) and \(\dfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\psi\), equal when \(\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m\), which is just \(E = p^2/2m\).
  4. With a force, energy is \(E = p^2/2m + V(x)\). Add \(V\psi\) on the right, and use all three directions: that is the equation above.
  5. Why the \(i\)? A real equation like the heat equation would make \(\psi\) die away. The \(i\) makes the wave oscillate and keeps total probability fixed (proved in the next card).

Check with numbers

A free electron with \(E = 1.00\) eV: \(\omega = E/\hbar = 1.602\times10^{-19}/1.055\times10^{-34} = 1.52\times10^{15}\) rad/s and \(k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar = 5.12\times10^9\) m⁻¹. Its wavelength \(2\pi/k = 1.23\) nm agrees with the de Broglie formula \(1.226/\sqrt{1}\) nm.

Born rule, normalisation and conservation

|ψ|² tells you where the particle is likely to be found. The Schrödinger equation never creates or destroys that probability; it only moves it around.

$$P(a \lt x \lt b) = \int_a^b |\psi|^2\,dx, \qquad \frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial j}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad j = \frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\psi^*\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)$$

Symbols

\(P\)
probability of finding the particle between \(a\) and \(b\), no unit
\(|\psi|^2 = \psi^*\psi\)
probability density, m⁻¹ in 1D
\(\psi^*\)
complex conjugate of \(\psi\)
\(j\)
probability current: probability flowing past a point per second, s⁻¹ in 1D

Derivation

The Born rule itself is a postulate (Max Born, 1926). What can be derived is that it is consistent: total probability stays exactly 1 for all time.

  1. Write \(\rho = \psi^*\psi\). Then \(\partial_t\rho = \psi^*\,\partial_t\psi + \psi\,\partial_t\psi^*\).
  2. From the Schrödinger equation, \(\partial_t\psi = \dfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\psi'' - \dfrac{i}{\hbar}V\psi\). Its conjugate is \(\partial_t\psi^* = -\dfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\psi^{*\prime\prime} + \dfrac{i}{\hbar}V\psi^*\) (\(V\) is real).
  3. Substitute. The \(V\) terms cancel: $$\partial_t\rho = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\psi^*\psi'' - \psi\psi^{*\prime\prime}\right) = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\psi^*\psi' - \psi\psi^{*\prime}\right).$$ (Differentiate the bracket to check: the \(\psi^{*\prime}\psi'\) terms cancel.)
  4. Since \(\psi^*\psi' - \psi\psi^{*\prime} = 2i\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')\), this is \(\partial_t\rho = -\partial_x j\) with \(j = \dfrac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')\): the continuity equation, the same form as for flowing water or charge.
  5. Integrate over all \(x\): \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\displaystyle\int\rho\,dx = -\big[j\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0\), because \(\psi\) vanishes far away. If \(\int|\psi|^2dx = 1\) at the start, it stays 1 forever.

Check with numbers

For a plane wave \(Ae^{ikx}\): \(\psi^*\psi' = ik|A|^2\), so \(j = |A|^2\,\hbar k/m = \rho\,v\), density times speed, as for a flowing fluid. For the 1 eV electron above, \(v = \hbar k/m = (1.055\times10^{-34})(5.12\times10^9)/(9.109\times10^{-31}) = 5.93\times10^5\) m/s, the same as \(\sqrt{2E/m}\).

See it in 3D: total probability stays at 1, then measure

Stationary states

States with a definite energy only spin their phase as time passes. Their probability cloud never changes, which is why atoms are stable.

$$\psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \phi(\mathbf{r})\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}, \qquad \hat{H}\phi = E\phi$$

Symbols

\(\phi(\mathbf{r})\)
spatial part of the wavefunction
\(E\)
energy of the state, J
\(\hat{H}\phi = E\phi\)
time-independent Schrödinger equation

Derivation

  1. Try a solution that is a product of a space part and a time part: \(\psi = \phi(\mathbf{r})\,T(t)\), with \(V\) not depending on time.
  2. Substitute and divide by \(\phi T\): $$i\hbar\,\frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{1}{\phi}\hat{H}\phi.$$
  3. The left side depends only on \(t\), the right only on \(\mathbf{r}\). For them to be equal everywhere and always, both must equal the same constant. Call it \(E\).
  4. Time part: \(dT/dt = -(iE/\hbar)T\), so \(T = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\). Space part: \(\hat{H}\phi = E\phi\).
  5. The probability density is \(|\psi|^2 = |\phi|^2\,|e^{-iEt/\hbar}|^2 = |\phi|^2\): constant in time. Only superpositions of different energies change shape (see changing energy level).
  6. Because the equation is linear, any sum \(\sum_n c_n\phi_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\) is also a solution, and every solution can be written this way.

Check with numbers

Hydrogen's 1s state (\(E = -13.6\) eV) turns its phase at \(\omega = |E|/\hbar = 13.6 \times 1.602\times10^{-19}/1.055\times10^{-34} = 2.07\times10^{16}\) rad/s, yet its cloud is perfectly still.

A spreading wave packet

A localised particle is a bundle of waves. The bundle moves at the classical speed, but it also spreads out, faster the tighter it started.

$$\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}\right)^2}, \qquad \langle x\rangle = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m}\,t$$

Symbols

\(\sigma_0\)
starting width (standard deviation of position), m
\(\sigma(t)\)
width at time \(t\), m
\(k_0\)
central wavenumber, m⁻¹; mean momentum is \(\hbar k_0\)
\(\langle x\rangle\)
centre of the packet, m
\(\phi(k)\)
amount of each plane wave \(e^{ikx}\) in the packet

Derivation

  1. Start with a Gaussian packet, \(\psi(x,0) \propto e^{-x^2/4\sigma_0^2}\,e^{ik_0x}\). As a sum of plane waves it is \(\psi(x,0) \propto \int \phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk\) with \(\phi(k) \propto e^{-\sigma_0^2(k-k_0)^2}\).
  2. Each plane wave is a stationary state of a free particle with \(E = \hbar^2k^2/2m\), so it just gains the phase \(e^{-i\hbar k^2 t/2m}\): $$\psi(x,t) \propto \int e^{-\sigma_0^2(k-k_0)^2 + ikx - i\hbar k^2t/2m}\,dk.$$
  3. Collect the powers of \(k\) in the exponent: \(-\alpha k^2 + \beta k - \sigma_0^2k_0^2\), with \(\alpha = \sigma_0^2 + \dfrac{i\hbar t}{2m}\) and \(\beta = 2\sigma_0^2k_0 + ix\).
  4. Gaussian integral: \(\int e^{-\alpha k^2 + \beta k}\,dk = \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}\,e^{\beta^2/4\alpha}\). Completing the square and simplifying (with \(v = \hbar k_0/m\)) gives $$\psi(x,t) \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x - vt)^2}{4\alpha}\right]e^{i(k_0x - \hbar k_0^2t/2m)}.$$
  5. Square the modulus. Since \(\operatorname{Re}(1/\alpha) = \sigma_0^2/|\alpha|^2\), $$|\psi|^2 \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-vt)^2\,\sigma_0^2}{2|\alpha|^2}\right] = \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x - vt)^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\right],$$ with \(\sigma(t)^2 = |\alpha|^2/\sigma_0^2 = \sigma_0^2 + \left(\dfrac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0}\right)^2\). That is the formula above.
  6. So the centre moves at \(v = \hbar k_0/m = p/m\), exactly as Newton says, while the width grows. The spreading timescale is \(\tau = 2m\sigma_0^2/\hbar\).

Check with numbers

An electron with \(\sigma_0 = 0.10\) nm: \(\tau = 2(9.109\times10^{-31})(10^{-20})/1.055\times10^{-34} = 1.7\times10^{-16}\) s. After 1 fs it is \(0.10\sqrt{1 + (10^{-15}/1.7\times10^{-16})^2} \approx 0.59\) nm wide. A 1 μg dust grain with \(\sigma_0 = 1\) μm has \(\tau \approx 2\times10^{13}\) s, about 600 000 years.

Tunnelling through a barrier

A particle can get through a wall it does not have the energy to climb. The chance falls off exponentially with the wall's thickness.

$$T = \left[1 + \frac{V_0^2\,\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0 - E)}\right]^{-1} \;\approx\; 16\,\frac{E}{V_0}\left(1 - \frac{E}{V_0}\right)e^{-2\kappa a}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}}{\hbar}$$

Symbols

\(T\)
transmission probability, no unit
\(E\)
particle energy, J, with \(E \lt V_0\)
\(V_0\)
barrier height, J
\(a\)
barrier thickness, m
\(k\)
\(\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\), wavenumber outside the barrier, m⁻¹
\(\kappa\)
decay constant inside the barrier, m⁻¹
\(r,\ t\)
reflected and transmitted amplitudes

Derivation

  1. The barrier: \(V = V_0\) for \(0 \lt x \lt a\) and \(V = 0\) elsewhere. Solve \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\) in each region. Outside, waves \(e^{\pm ikx}\); inside, since \(E \lt V_0\), growing and decaying exponentials \(e^{\pm\kappa x}\): $$\psi = \begin{cases} e^{ikx} + r\,e^{-ikx} & x \lt 0 \\ C e^{\kappa x} + D e^{-\kappa x} & 0 \lt x \lt a \\ t\,e^{ikx} & x \gt a \end{cases}$$
  2. \(\psi\) and \(d\psi/dx\) must be continuous at \(x = 0\) and \(x = a\) (otherwise \(\psi''\) would be infinite). At \(x = a\): $$Ce^{\kappa a} + De^{-\kappa a} = te^{ika},\qquad \kappa\left(Ce^{\kappa a} - De^{-\kappa a}\right) = ik\,te^{ika},$$ which give \(C = \dfrac{\kappa + ik}{2\kappa}\,t\,e^{ika}e^{-\kappa a}\) and \(D = \dfrac{\kappa - ik}{2\kappa}\,t\,e^{ika}e^{\kappa a}\).
  3. At \(x = 0\): \(1 + r = C + D\) and \(ik(1 - r) = \kappa(C - D)\). Multiply the first by \(ik\) and add the second to remove \(r\): \(2ik = (\kappa + ik)C - (\kappa - ik)D\).
  4. Substitute \(C\) and \(D\): $$\frac{1}{t} = \frac{e^{ika}}{4ik\kappa}\left[(\kappa + ik)^2e^{-\kappa a} - (\kappa - ik)^2e^{\kappa a}\right] = e^{ika}\left[\cosh\kappa a + i\,\frac{\kappa^2 - k^2}{2k\kappa}\sinh\kappa a\right].$$
  5. Take the modulus squared and use \(\cosh^2 = 1 + \sinh^2\): $$\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{|t|^2} = 1 + \sinh^2\kappa a\left[1 + \frac{(\kappa^2 - k^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\right] = 1 + \frac{(k^2 + \kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2\kappa a.$$
  6. With \(\hbar^2k^2 = 2mE\) and \(\hbar^2\kappa^2 = 2m(V_0 - E)\), \(\dfrac{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2} = \dfrac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0 - E)}\). That is the exact result.
  7. Thick barrier (\(\kappa a \gg 1\)): \(\sinh\kappa a \approx \tfrac12 e^{\kappa a}\) and the 1 is negligible, giving the approximate form. This is an approximation, good once \(\kappa a \gtrsim 2\).

Check with numbers

An electron with \(E = 1.0\) eV meets a barrier \(V_0 = 2.0\) eV, \(a = 0.50\) nm thick. \(\kappa = \sqrt{2m(1.0\ \text{eV})}/\hbar = 5.12\times10^9\) m⁻¹, so \(\kappa a = 2.56\) and \(\sinh^2(2.56) = 41.5\). \(V_0^2/4E(V_0-E) = 1\), so \(T = 1/42.5 = 2.4\%\). Double the thickness to 1.0 nm and \(T\) falls to \(1.4\times10^{-4}\): 170 times smaller. Scanning tunnelling microscopes use this steep fall to sense single atoms.

Quantum harmonic oscillator

A mass on a spring, made quantum, has evenly spaced energy levels and can never be completely still.

$$E_n = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Symbols

\(\omega\)
classical angular frequency \(\sqrt{k_s/m}\), rad/s
\(\hat H\)
\(\dfrac{\hat p^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2\hat x^2\)
\(\hat a,\ \hat a^\dagger\)
lowering and raising (ladder) operators
\(n\)
number of energy quanta in the oscillator

Derivation

  1. Define \(\hat a = \sqrt{\dfrac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x + \dfrac{i\hat p}{m\omega}\right)\) and its partner \(\hat a^\dagger = \sqrt{\dfrac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x - \dfrac{i\hat p}{m\omega}\right)\).
  2. Multiply out, using \([\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar\) from the uncertainty card: $$\hat a^\dagger\hat a = \frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\left(\hat x^2 + \frac{\hat p^2}{m^2\omega^2} + \frac{i}{m\omega}[\hat x,\hat p]\right) = \frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega} - \frac12.$$ So \(\hat H = \hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a + \tfrac12\right)\). The same algebra gives \([\hat a,\hat a^\dagger] = 1\).
  3. Then \([\hat H,\hat a] = -\hbar\omega\,\hat a\). If \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\), then \(\hat H(\hat a\psi) = (E - \hbar\omega)(\hat a\psi)\): \(\hat a\) steps down the ladder by exactly \(\hbar\omega\), and \(\hat a^\dagger\) steps up.
  4. The energy cannot go below zero: \(\langle\hat H\rangle = \hbar\omega\left(\|\hat a\psi\|^2 + \tfrac12\right) \ge \tfrac12\hbar\omega\). So the ladder must stop at a bottom state with \(\hat a\psi_0 = 0\), which has \(E_0 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega\).
  5. Climbing up \(n\) rungs: \(E_n = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega\).
  6. The ground state itself: \(\hat a\psi_0 = 0\) is \(x\psi_0 + \dfrac{\hbar}{m\omega}\psi_0' = 0\), so \(\psi_0 \propto e^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}\), a Gaussian.

Check with numbers

The H–Cl bond vibrates at \(f = 8.66\times10^{13}\) Hz, so its levels are \(hf = 6.626\times10^{-34} \times 8.66\times10^{13} = 5.74\times10^{-20}\) J \(= 0.358\) eV apart. HCl therefore absorbs infrared light at \(\lambda = 1240/0.358 = 3.46\) μm, which is exactly where its absorption band is.

4Hydrogen and light

Solve the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen, then ask what happens when light shines on it. This is how atoms absorb and emit photons.

Hydrogen, solved

Plug a simple guess into the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen and out drop Bohr's radius and the 13.6 eV ionisation energy, with no orbits assumed.

$$\psi_{1s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}\,e^{-r/a_0}, \qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2},\quad l = 0,\dots,n-1,\quad m_l = -l,\dots,l$$

Symbols

\(r\)
distance from the proton, m
\(a_0\)
Bohr radius, 0.0529 nm
\(n,\ l,\ m_l\)
principal, orbital and magnetic quantum numbers
\(V(r)\)
\(-ke^2/r\), Coulomb potential energy, J

Derivation

  1. For a spherically symmetric state, \(\nabla^2\psi = \psi'' + \dfrac{2}{r}\psi'\). The time-independent equation is $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\left(\psi'' + \frac{2}{r}\psi'\right) - \frac{ke^2}{r}\psi = E\psi.$$
  2. Guess \(\psi = e^{-r/a}\) for some length \(a\). Then \(\psi' = -\psi/a\) and \(\psi'' = \psi/a^2\).
  3. Substitute and divide by \(\psi\): $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e a^2} + \left(\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e a} - ke^2\right)\frac{1}{r} = E.$$
  4. The right side is a constant, so the \(1/r\) term must vanish for every \(r\): \(\dfrac{\hbar^2}{m_e a} = ke^2\), so \(a = \dfrac{\hbar^2}{m_e ke^2} = a_0\), Bohr's radius.
  5. What is left is the energy: \(E = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e a_0^2} = -\dfrac{ke^2}{2a_0} = -13.6\) eV.
  6. Normalise: \(\int_0^\infty e^{-2r/a_0}\,4\pi r^2\,dr = \pi a_0^3\), so the constant is \(1/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}\).
  7. The general solution (separating \(\psi = R(r)\,Y_l^{m}(\theta,\phi)\) and requiring \(\psi\) to stay finite) only works when \(E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2\), with \(l\) from 0 to \(n - 1\) and \(m_l\) from \(-l\) to \(l\). That is \(n^2\) orbitals per level, \(2n^2\) electrons with spin. The full series solution is a university topic; the result is shown here.

Check with numbers

\(ke^2 = 1.440\) eV·nm, so \(ke^2/2a_0 = 1.440/(2 \times 0.0529) = 13.6\) eV. The most likely distance for the 1s electron, where \(4\pi r^2|\psi|^2\) peaks, is at \(\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(r^2e^{-2r/a_0}\right) = 0\), so \(r = a_0\).

Quantised angular momentum

An electron's orbital angular momentum has a fixed size set by l, and only 2l + 1 allowed directions.

$$|\mathbf{L}| = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar, \qquad L_z = m_l\hbar,\quad m_l = -l, \dots, +l$$

Symbols

\(\mathbf L\)
orbital angular momentum, J·s
\(L_z\)
its component along the z-axis, J·s
\(l,\ m_l\)
orbital and magnetic quantum numbers
\(\hat L_\pm\)
\(\hat L_x \pm i\hat L_y\), ladder operators

Derivation

  1. From \(\hat{\mathbf L} = \hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf p}\) and \([\hat x,\hat p_x] = i\hbar\) one finds \([\hat L_x,\hat L_y] = i\hbar\hat L_z\) (and cyclic). So \(\hat L_z\) and \(\hat L^2\) can be sharp together, but not two components.
  2. Then \([\hat L_z,\hat L_\pm] = \pm\hbar\hat L_\pm\): \(\hat L_+\) raises \(L_z\) by \(\hbar\), \(\hat L_-\) lowers it, without changing \(L^2\).
  3. \(L_z^2 \le L^2\), so the ladder has a top state with \(\hat L_+\psi_{\text{top}} = 0\). Call its \(L_z = l\hbar\).
  4. Use \(\hat L^2 = \hat L_-\hat L_+ + \hat L_z^2 + \hbar\hat L_z\) on the top state: \(L^2 = 0 + l^2\hbar^2 + l\hbar^2 = l(l+1)\hbar^2\).
  5. By the same argument the bottom rung is at \(-l\hbar\), reached in whole steps, so \(2l\) is a whole number. For orbital motion the wavefunction \(e^{im_l\phi}\) must repeat after one turn, so \(l\) and \(m_l\) are integers. (Spin, with \(l = \tfrac12\), is the half-integer case.)

Check with numbers

A p electron (\(l = 1\)): \(|\mathbf L| = \sqrt2\,\hbar = 1.49\times10^{-34}\) J·s, with \(L_z = -\hbar, 0, +\hbar\). Even at \(m_l = +1\), the angle to the z-axis is \(\cos^{-1}(1/\sqrt2) = 45^\circ\): the vector can never point straight along z.

Spin measurements

Measure spin along any direction and you get only up or down. If it was up along z, the chance of up along a tilted direction is cos²(θ/2).

$$P(\text{up along } \hat n) = \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}, \qquad |{\uparrow_{\hat n}}\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|{\uparrow}\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|{\downarrow}\rangle$$

Symbols

\(\theta\)
angle between the z-axis and the new measuring direction \(\hat n\)
\(\varphi\)
azimuthal angle of \(\hat n\)
\(|{\uparrow}\rangle,\ |{\downarrow}\rangle\)
spin up and down along z, \(S_z = \pm\tfrac12\hbar\)
\(\boldsymbol\sigma\)
Pauli matrices; spin is \(\tfrac{\hbar}{2}\boldsymbol\sigma\)

Derivation

  1. Spin along \(\hat n = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)\) is measured by \(\tfrac\hbar2\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), with $$\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\,e^{-i\varphi}\\ \sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi} & -\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$
  2. Check that \(\left(\cos\tfrac\theta2,\ e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\right)\) is the "up" eigenvector with eigenvalue \(+1\). Top row: \(\cos\theta\cos\tfrac\theta2 + \sin\theta\sin\tfrac\theta2 = \cos\tfrac\theta2\left(\cos\theta + 2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\right) = \cos\tfrac\theta2\), using \(\sin\theta = 2\sin\tfrac\theta2\cos\tfrac\theta2\) and \(\cos\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\). The bottom row works the same way.
  3. Born rule: an atom prepared up along z is found up along \(\hat n\) with probability \(|\langle{\uparrow_{\hat n}}|{\uparrow}\rangle|^2 = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2\).

Check with numbers

Magnet tilted by 60°: \(\cos^2 30^\circ = 0.75\), so 75% go up and 25% down. At 90°: \(\cos^2 45^\circ = 0.5\), a coin toss. At 180° (upside down): 0%. A classical spinning magnet would instead give a smear of deflections.

Changing energy level: the oscillating cloud

While an atom is partway between two levels, its electron cloud sloshes back and forth at exactly the frequency of the light it absorbs or emits.

$$|\psi|^2 = |c_1|^2\phi_1^2 + |c_2|^2\phi_2^2 + 2|c_1c_2|\,\phi_1\phi_2\cos(\omega t - \delta), \qquad \omega = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar}$$

Symbols

\(\phi_1,\ \phi_2\)
lower and upper stationary states (real orbitals such as 1s and 2pz)
\(c_1,\ c_2\)
complex amplitudes, \(|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2 = 1\)
\(\delta\)
relative phase of \(c_2\) and \(c_1\)
\(E_1,\ E_2\)
energies of the two levels, J
\(z_{12}\)
dipole matrix element \(\int\phi_1\,z\,\phi_2\,dV\), m

Derivation

  1. From stationary states, a mix of two levels evolves as $$\psi = c_1\phi_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} + c_2\phi_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}.$$
  2. Multiply by its conjugate. The squared terms are steady; the cross terms carry the phase difference \(e^{\pm i(E_2 - E_1)t/\hbar}\): $$|\psi|^2 = |c_1|^2\phi_1^2 + |c_2|^2\phi_2^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(c_1^*c_2\,e^{-i\omega t}\right)\phi_1\phi_2.$$ Writing \(c_1^*c_2 = |c_1c_2|e^{i\delta}\) gives the cosine.
  3. The cloud's centre of charge: \(\langle z\rangle = \int z|\psi|^2dV\). The steady terms give zero for orbitals that are symmetric about the nucleus, leaving $$\langle z\rangle(t) = 2|c_1c_2|\,z_{12}\cos(\omega t - \delta).$$
  4. An electron cloud whose centre moves up and down is an oscillating electric dipole, like a tiny radio aerial. It couples to light of angular frequency \(\omega\), so \(hf = \hbar\omega = E_2 - E_1\): Bohr's frequency condition, now explained rather than assumed.
  5. The dipole is biggest when \(|c_1| = |c_2| = 1/\sqrt2\) and zero in a pure stationary state, which is why an atom sitting in one level does not radiate classically. (Spontaneous emission from the pure upper level needs quantum electrodynamics; this picture explains absorption and stimulated emission.)

Check with numbers

1s ↔ 2p in hydrogen: \(E_2 - E_1 = 13.6(1 - \tfrac14) = 10.2\) eV, so \(\omega = 10.2 \times 1.602\times10^{-19}/1.055\times10^{-34} = 1.55\times10^{16}\) rad/s, \(f = 2.47\times10^{15}\) Hz and \(\lambda = c/f = 121.5\) nm, the Lyman-α line. At an equal mix the cloud's centre swings \(\pm z_{12} = \pm0.745\,a_0 = \pm0.039\) nm.

Rabi oscillations

Shine steady light at the right colour on an atom and it does not just jump up: it swings smoothly up and down between the two levels.

$$P_2(t) = \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega'^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega' t}{2}\right), \qquad \Omega' = \sqrt{\Omega^2 + \Delta^2}$$

Symbols

\(P_2(t)\)
probability of finding the atom in the upper level
\(\Omega\)
Rabi frequency, \(eE_0z_{12}/\hbar\): how strongly the light couples the levels, rad/s
\(E_0\)
amplitude of the light's electric field, V/m
\(\omega_L,\ \omega_0\)
light's angular frequency and the atom's \((E_2 - E_1)/\hbar\)
\(\Delta\)
detuning \(\omega_L - \omega_0\), rad/s
\(\Omega'\)
generalised Rabi frequency, rad/s

Derivation

  1. Light along z adds the energy \(eE_0z\cos\omega_Lt\). Write \(\psi = c_1(t)\phi_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} + c_2(t)\phi_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\) and put it into the Schrödinger equation. Projecting onto \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\) (using \(z_{11} = z_{22} = 0\)) gives $$i\dot c_1 = \Omega\cos(\omega_Lt)\,e^{-i\omega_0t}\,c_2,\qquad i\dot c_2 = \Omega\cos(\omega_Lt)\,e^{i\omega_0t}\,c_1.$$
  2. Write \(\cos\omega_Lt = \tfrac12(e^{i\omega_Lt} + e^{-i\omega_Lt})\). Each equation gets a slow term \(e^{\pm i\Delta t}\) and a fast term \(e^{\pm i(\omega_L + \omega_0)t}\). Rotating-wave approximation: the fast term averages to nearly zero, so drop it (good when \(\Omega,|\Delta| \ll \omega_0\)): $$i\dot c_1 = \frac{\Omega}{2}e^{i\Delta t}c_2,\qquad i\dot c_2 = \frac{\Omega}{2}e^{-i\Delta t}c_1.$$
  3. Differentiate the second equation and use the first to remove \(c_1\): $$\ddot c_2 + i\Delta\,\dot c_2 + \frac{\Omega^2}{4}c_2 = 0.$$
  4. Try \(c_2 = e^{st}\): \(s^2 + i\Delta s + \Omega^2/4 = 0\), so \(s = -\tfrac{i\Delta}{2} \pm \tfrac{i\Omega'}{2}\) with \(\Omega' = \sqrt{\Omega^2 + \Delta^2}\).
  5. Start in the lower level: \(c_2(0) = 0\), and from step 2, \(\dot c_2(0) = -i\Omega/2\). The solution is $$c_2(t) = -\frac{i\Omega}{\Omega'}\,e^{-i\Delta t/2}\sin\frac{\Omega't}{2},$$ and \(P_2 = |c_2|^2\) is the formula above.
  6. On resonance (\(\Delta = 0\)), \(P_2 = \sin^2(\Omega t/2)\): a pulse lasting \(t = \pi/\Omega\) (a "π pulse") flips the atom completely. Off resonance, the swing is faster but never complete.

Check with numbers

With \(\Omega = 2\pi \times 10\) MHz, one full Rabi cycle takes \(2\pi/\Omega = 100\) ns and a π pulse 50 ns. Detune by \(\Delta = \Omega\): \(\Omega' = \sqrt2\,\Omega\), so the atom reaches the upper level with at most 50% probability, and cycles \(\sqrt2\) times faster.

Selection rules

Light can only drive jumps where l changes by exactly one. Some jumps, like 1s to 2s, are forbidden however bright the light.

$$\Delta l = \pm1, \qquad z_{12} = \int\phi_1\,z\,\phi_2\,dV; \qquad z_{1s,2p_z} = \frac{128\sqrt2}{243}\,a_0 \approx 0.745\,a_0, \quad z_{1s,2s} = 0$$

Symbols

\(\Delta l\)
change in the orbital quantum number
\(z_{12}\)
dipole matrix element, m; the Rabi frequency is proportional to it
parity
whether a function is even or odd under \(\mathbf r \to -\mathbf r\); orbitals have parity \((-1)^l\)

Derivation

  1. From the Rabi card, light can only couple two levels if \(z_{12} \ne 0\).
  2. Turn every point through the nucleus, \(\mathbf r \to -\mathbf r\). The volume integral is unchanged, but \(z \to -z\), and each orbital picks up \((-1)^l\). So \(z_{12} = (-1)^{l_1 + l_2 + 1}z_{12}\).
  3. If \(l_1 + l_2\) is even, \(z_{12} = -z_{12}\), so \(z_{12} = 0\). Only odd changes in \(l\) survive. The photon carries one unit of angular momentum, which rules out \(|\Delta l| \ge 3\), leaving \(\Delta l = \pm1\). (The \(\phi\) integral also forces \(\Delta m_l = 0\) for light polarised along z.)
  4. 1s → 2s: both have \(l = 0\), so \(z_{12} = 0\). Forbidden.
  5. 1s → 2pz: with \(\phi_{1s} = \dfrac{e^{-r/a_0}}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}\) and \(\phi_{2p_z} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{32\pi a_0^3}}\dfrac{r}{a_0}e^{-r/2a_0}\cos\theta\), $$z_{12} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{32}\,\pi a_0^4}\underbrace{\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi}_{4\pi/3}\ \underbrace{\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-3r/2a_0}\,dr}_{4!\,(2a_0/3)^5}.$$
  6. \(4!\,(2/3)^5 = 768/243\), so \(z_{12} = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\cdot\dfrac{768}{243}\cdot\dfrac{a_0}{\sqrt{32}\,\pi} = \dfrac{256}{243\sqrt2}\,a_0 = \dfrac{128\sqrt2}{243}\,a_0\).

Check with numbers

\(128\sqrt2/243 = 181.0/243 = 0.745\), so \(z_{12} = 0.745 \times 0.0529 = 0.0394\) nm. Because 1s → 2s is forbidden, an electron put in 2s cannot drop straight down by emitting one photon. It lives about 0.12 s (it decays by emitting two photons), against 1.6 ns for 2p: about 75 million times longer.

5Qubits

Any two-level system, a spin, an atom's two levels or a superconducting circuit, is a qubit. One picture describes them all.

The Bloch sphere

Every possible state of a qubit is one point on the surface of a sphere. The north pole is |0⟩, the south pole is |1⟩, and everything else is a superposition.

$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \pi,\ 0 \le \varphi \lt 2\pi$$

Symbols

\(|0\rangle,\ |1\rangle\)
the two basis states (spin up/down, lower/upper level)
\(\theta\)
polar angle on the sphere
\(\varphi\)
azimuthal angle: the relative phase of the two parts

Derivation

  1. A general state is \(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\): two complex numbers, four real numbers.
  2. Normalisation, \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\), removes one.
  3. An overall phase changes nothing measurable: for any state \(\chi\), \(|\langle\chi|e^{i\gamma}\psi\rangle|^2 = |e^{i\gamma}|^2|\langle\chi|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle\chi|\psi\rangle|^2\). So choose \(\gamma\) to make \(\alpha\) real and positive. That removes another, leaving two real numbers: a point on a sphere.
  4. With \(\alpha\) real and \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\), write \(\alpha = \cos(\theta/2)\) and \(\beta = e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)\).
  5. Why half-angles? \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) cannot both be found (they are orthogonal), yet they should sit at opposite poles, \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\). Half-angles turn "opposite on the sphere" into "orthogonal": \(\langle 0|1\rangle = 0\) at \(\pi/2\) in the state space, \(\pi\) on the sphere.
  6. The point's position is the average spin direction: \(\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta)\). This is the same as the spin card, with \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\) as real directions in space.

Check with numbers

On the equator, \(\theta = 90^\circ\), \(\varphi = 0\): \(|\psi\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt2\), so \(P(0) = \cos^2 45^\circ = 0.5\). At \(\theta = 60^\circ\): \(P(0) = \cos^2 30^\circ = 0.75\). A π pulse in the Rabi card moves the point from the north pole to the south pole.
